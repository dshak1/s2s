Executive summary
Steppe to Screen should be a “shared expedition” rather than a quiz platform: a projected cultural journey where phones act as simple controllers, vocabulary tools, and creative artifacts—not separate isolated lesson screens.

The strongest reusable pattern is a short multimodal loop: hear a native-speaker word → connect it to a drawing/photo/action → say, tap, sort, or move it → see an immediate story-world consequence. Duolingo ABC, Drops, and early-literacy games all use variants of this; Duolingo ABC also supports offline use.

Keep your existing runner, but make speech optional, low-stakes, and group-supported. Children’s voice-interface research emphasizes feedback and dialogue design; classroom noise, young voices, accent variation, and Kazakh ASR limitations make strict pass/fail speech gates risky.

In workshops, borrow Kahoot’s “one shared screen, many simple devices” structure—but not its speed-ranking culture. Kahoot-style rounds reward rapid answering; this can turn a language/culture activity into reflex trivia.

Use cooperative team missions, hidden-object searching, call-and-response, and collective map-building as primary workshop mechanics. Reserve head-to-head races for occasional 30–60 second energizers.

For ages 5–7, reduce reading load: use clear imagery, spoken prompts, large tap targets, repeatable routines, and one decision at a time. Child-design research likewise reports teacher requests for colors/icons and less text for early readers.

Existing Kazakh options appear oriented toward adults or general beginners: Mango provides conversational phrases, grammar notes, phonetics, and narration; Soyle is positioned as traditional grammar/reading; 50LANGUAGES offers downloadable audio/offline lessons. The gap is a native-speaker-reviewed, child-first, workshop-first Kazakh experience.

Copy retrieval, visual association, micro-sessions, immediate feedback, and content sequencing from Duolingo/Drops. Avoid leagues, streak pressure, excessive currencies, long answer grids, and treating culture as disconnected trivia.

Build an authoring pathway early: educators and native speakers should be able to turn a word into a usable “content card” with audio, image/drawing, RU/EN bridge, acceptable variants, a movement prompt, and cultural context. This compounds your existing human labeling pipeline.

For the next eight weeks, prioritize five things: offline lesson packs; shared projector missions; audio-first picture retrieval; facilitator-controlled cooperative rounds; and a speech-to-action mode with graceful fallback. Do not attempt full live multiplayer infrastructure, open UGC, or AR.

Kazakh and low-resource language findings
What current offerings do well
Mango Languages provides conversational orientation: listen again at conversational speed, phonetics, articulated narration, grammar notes, and color coding. This is useful inspiration for the RU/EN bridge and for making native audio inspectable—not just playable.

50LANGUAGES explicitly supports offline learning, downloadable MP3 audio, and tests/games. Its core lesson structure is a useful proof that downloadable audio packs are feasible and valuable for Kazakh learners.

Qazaq App reportedly used topical chapters, word/phrase lessons, pictures, and country/history material. That combination—language plus local context—is directionally aligned with Steppe to Screen.

Soyle.kz fills a public-service role, providing grammar, vocabulary, reading, listening, and media; community commentary characterizes it as traditional grammar/reading oriented.

Community discussion consistently points people toward Mango, Soyle, and 50LANGUAGES because mainstream Duolingo and Babbel do not offer Kazakh. That is an opportunity, but it also means you should not infer that demand automatically equals child-product fit.

Transferable prototypes and patterns
Most transferable
MathKingdom’s voice-agent game pattern
This voice-based game for ages 4–7 is relevant because it treats spoken interaction as part of a game rather than a separate microphone test. Transfer the principle—not the full conversational agent: use bounded spoken utterances tied to visible actions. For example, say алма and the apple cart moves; if recognition is uncertain, the cart still moves after a second try or a partner tap.

Around the World in 70 Frames
This ShellHacks project combines language learning, intelligent NPCs, and speech recognition. Its transferable concept is “speech with a narrative purpose”: the child speaks to progress through a travel/cultural setting. Do not copy the open-ended NPC stack for the MVP; build scripted, finite dialogue turns instead.

RagamNesia
A Garuda Hacks project described as using digital folktales and games to teach local language and culture. The core insight is highly relevant: cultural heritage is not a themed vocabulary skin—it is the narrative container that determines which language children need.

Voice-interface design research for children
Research identifies implications for feedback and dialogue in voice interfaces used for children’s language learning. The practical design transfer: show what was heard, avoid binary “wrong” language, offer recasts, let children repeat, and always provide a nonvoice path.

Child co-design work
Studies of child-centered technology emphasize reducing textual load, using visual cues, and involving children/teachers in shaping activities. Run lightweight co-design: after each workshop, give children 3 visual votes—“want more,” “confusing,” “I want to make this.”

What to copy and avoid
Copy from Duolingo-class products
One skill per moment. A round should teach one target: identify the sound, choose the picture, say it, or use it in a mini-scene—not all four at once.

Multimodal repetition. Hear → see → touch → say is much stronger than translation → multiple choice. Duolingo ABC’s tracing, drag/drop, stories, read-aloud highlighting, and hands-on lessons are a strong early-childhood reference.

Immediate, informative feedback. Replay the audio, slow it down, surface an image clue, or model the phrase again. Avoid generic error buzzers.

Frequent familiar return. Build a small local “again soon” queue from missed or hesitant answers rather than an adult-looking deck. Drops’ core model is fast visual practice plus spaced repetition.

Character continuity. Your existing kid-drawn avatars can carry emotional continuity better than generic mascots—especially if their appearance represents a contribution rather than a randomized prize.

Deliberately avoid
Individual leaderboards and speed as the default. Real teacher discussions warn that gamification platforms can become entertainment-first and better suited to quick recall than deeper understanding.

Streaks, leagues, scarcity, loot-box-like rewards. These are not needed for a weekly workshop product and can displace intrinsic/cultural motivation.

Long linear skill trees. A journey should represent a place, story, season, or family visit—not a SaaS-style “Unit 4: 63% complete.”

Translation-first drilling. Let Kazakh audio, image, action, and situation lead. RU/EN should be an optional scaffold, not the core cognitive task.

Strict automated pronunciation grading. Kazakh child speech, bilingual transfer, noise, and ASR coverage make this an equity and trust risk.

Open chat, profiles, public galleries, or follower systems. They conflict with anonymous children and create moderation work you do not need.

Always-online live quiz dependency. Kahoot-style live sessions depend on connectivity; even community guidance notes an initial connection is needed to load content.

Suggested eight-week sequence
Weeks 1–2: Offline package manifest, service-worker caching, session recovery, facilitator preflight screen.

Weeks 2–3: Audio-first scene hunt and category sorting, with native-reviewed audio card format.

Weeks 3–4: Cooperative local round protocol: facilitator device/projector sends round state to nearby clients, with manual fallback.

Weeks 4–5: Journey map with three cultural scenes and one short interactive read-aloud per scene.

Weeks 5–6: Upgrade speech runner: confidence bands, repeat option, “we heard you” animation, tap-to-continue/partner support.

Weeks 6–7: Device-local retrieval queue for homework and a printable/QR workshop pack handoff if desired.

Weeks 7–8: Template postcard plus a cooperative “boss” finale; conduct two workshops and tune based on observed behavior.

Mini PRDs for the top five
1. Offline workshop packs
Player fantasy: “My device carries our expedition kit, even when the internet disappears.”

Three-minute loop

Facilitator selects a named workshop pack before children arrive.

Devices load the same local assets: audio, images, scenes, prompts, and answer rules.

Children complete one activity; progress is retained locally until an eventual sync or exported facilitator summary.

Log

Pack version and asset-cache success.

Device readiness before session.

Asset-load errors and time-to-first-round.

Completion by activity, without a personal identifier.

Anonymous ephemeral session/device token, rotated after the workshop.

Facilitator role: Run a preflight check, see which devices are ready, and trigger a “continue without network” state.

One-workshop success metric: At least 90% of participating devices can begin the first activity within 30 seconds after the facilitator starts, with no network-dependent blocker.

2. Audio-first scene hunt
Player fantasy: “I am a sharp-eyed explorer who understands what the guide says.”

Three-minute loop

A native speaker says one target word or short phrase.

Children tap the matching item in a rich illustrated scene.

The item animates and speaks its word again; a quick second prompt asks children to find another item or answer “what disappeared?”

After three correct group discoveries, the scene unlocks a tiny cultural fact, sound, or animation.

Log

Target item, first-tap correctness, retries, hint use, audio replay count.

Time-to-answer bucket, not precise per-child profiling.

Confusion pairs: which distractor images receive taps.

Age-band and workshop ID, if the facilitator chooses to provide them.

Facilitator role: Introduce the scene orally, pause after common confusion, and ask for a gesture or real-world object connection.

One-workshop success metric: At least 75% correct first attempts after one audio replay or fewer, plus at least 60% correct delayed recognition in a later scene.

3. Cooperative facilitator rounds
Player fantasy: “Our group is helping the caravan reach the next place.”

Three-minute loop

The projector shows a shared obstacle: bridge, market, yurt preparation, animal trail, or weather event.

The facilitator pushes one simple prompt to phones: choose, hear-and-tap, or sequence.

Correct aggregate answers add visible shared progress; incorrect attempts reveal a clue rather than deducting points.

The room completes the obstacle together and hears a short phrase tied to the next scene.

Log

Round start/end, participating-device count, aggregate accuracy.

Prompt type, response distribution, skipped responses.

Whether a facilitator revealed a hint/replayed audio.

Completion time and reconnect/dropout count.

Facilitator role: Choose pacing, replay audio, form ad hoc pairs, and narrate why the language mattered in the shared scene.

One-workshop success metric: At least 80% of active devices submit an answer in each round, while no child needs to wait more than 20 seconds between prompt and visible consequence.

4. Speech-to-action with fallback
Player fantasy: “My Kazakh voice makes the world move.”

Three-minute loop

The game models a word or phrase with a visual mouth/character cue and clean native audio.

The child says it; the runner, animal, boat, or character advances on high-confidence recognition.

If confidence is uncertain, the game says “Let’s try together,” replays slowly, and accepts a second try.

The child can choose “echo with a friend” or tap a speech-bubble button so no one is blocked.

Log

Prompt ID, recognition confidence band only, attempts, fallback choice.

Audio replay and slow-mode usage.

Completion path: voice / partner / tap.

Aggregate failure rate by device/browser and room noise rating entered by facilitator.

Facilitator role: Model the phrase, normalize retries, choose group echo mode during noisy periods, and never publicly display individual failures.

One-workshop success metric: At least 70% of children voluntarily attempt speech at least once, and fewer than 10% abandon a round due to recognition failure.

5. Cultural map missions
Player fantasy: “I travel through a living Kazakh world and help the people I meet.”

Three-minute loop

The map opens a place/story node: family home, steppe journey, market, celebration, seasonal landscape, or animal-care scene.

A character needs help using 3–5 target words.

Children complete two short activities—e.g., hear-and-find, then say-or-sort.

The place gains a visible artifact: a stamp, illustrated object, line of a story, or shared tapestry tile.

Log

Mission selected/completed, vocabulary targets, activity results.

Optional bridge-language hint use.

Return visits and delayed retrieval on subsequent workshops.

Native-review content version.

Facilitator role: Frame the scene, add lived context or regional nuance, and explicitly distinguish “one family/place’s practice” from universal claims about all Kazakh people.

One-workshop success metric: After the mission, at least 70% of children can identify 3 of 4 target words in a no-translation review round and can recall one contextual association.

Do not build
Always-on multiplayer with accounts, matchmaking, or persistent teams. It conflicts with weak Wi‑Fi, anonymous children, privacy minimization, and small-team operational capacity.

A public kid social feed, comments, or chat. It has low learning value relative to the moderation, safeguarding, identity, and retention burden.

Open-ended AI roleplay NPCs. They are costly, unreliable offline, difficult to constrain culturally and linguistically, and likely to frustrate young children when speech recognition fails.

Heavy AR scavenger hunts. They introduce permissions, device compatibility, camera/lighting constraints, safety issues, and setup time; research interest in AR does not make it suitable for your classroom constraints.

A generic flashcard app as the main product loop. Local spaced review is useful as a supporting homework mechanism; it should not replace workshop play, speech, story, or culture.

Global leaderboards, streak freezes, ranked leagues, and randomized reward economies. They create comparison and distraction. Teacher discussion specifically warns that platform gamification can prioritize fun over deeper learning.

Strict pronunciation scores displayed to children. Especially unsafe for ages 5–7 and for a low-resource language where recognition quality can vary.

Fully branching story trees. Content and native-speaker review complexity will grow faster than learning value. Use tight two-choice scenes with shared convergence.

Text-heavy grammar explanations for the core loop. Keep grammar as optional facilitator notes or older-child explainers tied to meaningful phrases.

Device-by-device adaptive learning profiles based on long-lived IDs. Anonymous local adaptation can be limited and transparent; deep personalization without accounts is technically and ethically mismatched.
