# Perplexity prompt: game formats kids can shape themselves

Use Deep Research mode. Act as a learning-game researcher, primary-school
teacher, child co-design facilitator, and senior web-game producer. I am
building Steppe to Screen, an offline-first web platform for children ages 5 to
12 learning Kazakh language and culture. It runs in weekly hybrid workshops:
one facilitator/projector, children on phones or tablets, homework between
sessions, and a small team of educators and native speakers reviewing content.

Research game formats that are materially more engaging than flash cards,
keyboard games, or repeated multiple choice. Prioritize formats where children
can customize the world, characters, rules, questions, stories, routes, art,
sound, or difficulty without needing to code. Include successful examples from
language learning, classroom games, UGC games, co-design with children,
physical-digital play, endless runners, rhythm games, drawing games, narrative
games, scavenger hunts, and collaborative projector games.

Answer these questions:

1. Which 12 game mechanics are strongest for teaching vocabulary,
   pronunciation, listening, speaking, sentence construction, and cultural
   knowledge to ages 5 to 12?
2. Which mechanics support genuine child authorship rather than superficial
   skin changes? Define a ladder from customizing a background to changing
   questions, rules, levels, and stories.
3. How could an endless-runner game work when a Kazakh vocabulary or listening
   decision appears before a fork, obstacle, shortcut, or power-up? Give a
   complete 3-minute play loop, progression system, failure/retry model, and
   accessibility alternatives.
4. What should a no-code game builder for children expose? Recommend the
   smallest useful set of templates, controls, constraints, and moderation
   gates. Include importing drawings exported from Canva or Figma, drawing on
   an in-app canvas, recording audio, and reusing homework.
5. How should weekly classes, homework, games, and the facilitator-led live
   session connect into one roadmap? Show the child loop, facilitator loop, and
   educator/native-speaker review loop.
6. How can the broader team contribute asynchronously? Cover proposing weekly
   packs, reviewing child-created questions, approving pronunciation, adding
   cultural context, and seeing evidence that their contribution was used.
7. What privacy, moderation, accessibility, offline, low-bandwidth, and
   low-resource-language risks matter for user-generated child content?
8. Which existing products or research projects are worth studying? Include
   direct links, screenshots or interface descriptions, and explain exactly
   what Steppe to Screen should borrow and what it should avoid.

For every recommendation, cite primary research, official product
documentation, or direct product examples. Distinguish evidence from your own
inference. Do not recommend a mechanic merely because it is popular.

Return:

- A comparison table of at least 12 mechanics with learning objective, ages,
  session length, solo/live mode, child-authorship depth, implementation effort,
  device requirements, offline feasibility, and main risk.
- The top 5 concepts ranked by learning value, replayability, child agency,
  workshop fit, and build cost.
- A detailed concept brief for the best endless-runner/fork game.
- A detailed concept brief for the best child game-builder.
- A 6-week roadmap connecting class, homework, solo play, live play, and team
  review.
- A production recommendation split into: build now, prototype next, research
  first, and reject.
- Five concrete usability-test tasks for children and five interview questions
  for facilitators.

Technical context: Next.js 16, React 19, TypeScript, Tailwind, Framer Motion,
Supabase, Vercel, localStorage-first, image/audio upload, existing recovery
codes, game telemetry, ticket review, and an AI investigation workflow. Assume
mobile portrait at 360px, tablets, desktop, and a classroom projector.
